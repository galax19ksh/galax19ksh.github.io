`In Dreams: The Enigmatic Lynchian World`
When I stumbled upon the October 1, 1990, issue of Time magazine featuring *David Lynch* as the star cover, I was immediately struck by its tagline: “Wild at Art.” It is a clever pun on the then-recently released *Nicolas Cage* and *Laura Dern* starrer, ***Wild at Heart (1990)***, which I always describe as *Lynch*’s *Romeo and Juliet*. That film premiered to a famously divided audience at Cannes but ended up winning the Palme d'Or (all thanks to jury president *Bernardo Bertolucci*'s controversial yet visionary decision at the festival). So, that tagline accompanied by his iconic pompadour and stern yet cordial eyes facing us, bathed in high-contrast hues, hit me with a sudden urge to write something about this “weird” filmmaker I have been deeply obsessed with over the years. The line *"This whole world is wild at heart and weird on top"*, delivered by *Dern*'s character *Lula* in a passionately somber Southern accent, accurately captures the bizarre and disturbing nature of Lynch's work. Enough has been said about the beloved late filmmaker's vastly influential oeuvre and the surrealist experimentalism he made mainstream, all of it purely a product of his singular creative process and otherworldly ethos, but I can still talk all day about him.
![Time cover lynch 1990](../media/David-Lynch-blog\Time-cover.jpg"){: width="350px" .align-center}
<p align="center"><sub><em>*David Lynch* on the cover of TIME, October 1, 1990</em></sub></p>

My introduction to *David Lynch* was ***Mulholland Drive (2001)***, which was a must-see in the “greatest mind-blowing movies of all time” lists I devoured during my late teens. The haunting Hollywood dreamscape drenched in uncompromising visual language did not help my visible frustration at the insanely puzzling plot. Looking for clues and motifs throughout to decode came to nothing (what the heck does the blue key even mean?). Clearly no different from the rest of his work, the psychosexual violence of ***Blue Velvet (1986)*** was more repulsive than my palate could handle at that time. Only after enough episodes of ***Twin Peaks (1990)*** and revisits to *Lynch*'s perversely tempting world did my understanding of his cinematic appeal evolve into something more than mere incoherent shock value or a fine case of acquired taste. This came as a revelation for my appreciation of art, especially surrealism, beyond the whole craze of hunting for “Rosetta stones”. Despite having joined the cultural lexicon, the usage of the adjective “Lynchian” never reached artists' work for me, proving a vision so singular that no one has ever come close to imitation or duplication.

A Romanticized Outlook on Life
=======
In the documentary ***It’s a Beautiful World (2014)*** by *Richard Beymer* (who plays *Ben Horne* in ***Twin Peaks (1990)***), when *David Lynch* was offered some snacks in the car along the India-Tibet border during his trip to India, he exclaimed, “I just had two cookies and a Coke!” with the same excitement as a breakthrough in the editing room. When you do something as minor as getting him a cup of coffee, he makes you feel like you have done the greatest thing in the world. Not some performative or isolated quirk, this sincere sense of appreciation for the littlest things in life apparently gives him a creative edge. Donning the earnestness of a 1950s Boy Scout, his one of a kinda persona - the white shirt buttoned to the collar without a tie, the silver quiff, the high-volume voice with a halting cadence - acts as a vessel for a highly romanticized, almost reverent engagement with the physical world. *Mel Brooks* famously dubbed him “*Jimmy Stewart* from Mars,” a moniker that perfectly encapsulates the juxtaposition of his macabre artistic visions and his profound, unwavering gratitude for the quotidian. FBI Special Agent *Dale Cooper*’s legendary, wide-eyed reverence for a “damn fine cup of coffee,” a slice of cherry pie, and the majestic Douglas firs is an uncanny translation of *Lynch*’s own ethos; even his alter ego, Bureau Chief *Gordon Cole*, shares his deafening volume and unabashed enthusiasm.

Since the beginning of the new millennium and the subsequent release of his final feature, ***Inland Empire (2006)***, an extremely skewed digital avant-garde opus, *Lynch* has drifted into a quieter retirement life, occupying himself with woodwork, experimental painting, and, most interestingly, with the internet through his website and weather reports. I remember, during my COVID-19 isolation, tuning in to his YouTube channel, where he would share a song in his mind, recite daily affirmations, and conduct his daily ritual of “swirling the numbers” to pick a number of the day from a glass jar. The deadpan delivery of daily weather reports from a particular room of his L.A. basement became a delightful comfort. From his weekly “It is a Friday once again!” to quinoa cooking sessions, *David*’s fascination with nature itself in the minute details apparently adds to his larger-than-life persona, which could be the very fundamental engine of his creative ritual. I truly believe his eccentric whimsy isn’t an escape from his art. In fact, I have realized that this oddball, idiosyncratic personality is almost as big a reason for my obsession with him as his body of work.

Surrealism as a Function of Creativity 
========
`*“Symbolism and meaning are two separate things. I think she found the right words by bypassing procedures like meaning and logic. She captured words in a dream, like delicately catching hold of a butterfly’s wings as it flutters around. Artists are those who can evade the verbose.” – Haruki Murakami*`

*David Lynch* has frequently said that words are inherently limiting. To verbalize a waking dream is to cage it, stripping it of its infinite, unspoken potential. While that explains his lifelong hesitation to talk about his work, his simple speaking vernacular apparently informs his indelible disdain for expository eloquence, but not out of a fear of reductive interpretation of the medium. We will never know if *David Lynch* ever “understood” most of his work in any literal, analytical sense. We will never know if any of the elements we call Lynchian - the abrupt eruptions of violence, the deadpan absurdity, the velvet-curtained dream sequences - are reverse-engineered from a carefully planned puzzle box. I certainly don’t believe so. Rather, in his book *Catching the Big Fish*, he describes a quiet and instinctive zone he calls the “Unified Field” that can be accessed through a practice of Transcendental Meditation (which he practiced twice daily and advocated for his whole life); the ideas seem to come organically from that ocean of pure consciousness. *“I really had this feeling that if there’s a Unified Field, there must be a unity between a Christmas tree bulb and this man from Poland who came in wearing these strange glasses. It’s interesting to see how these unrelated things live together,”* he said, recounting his shooting days of ***Inland Empire (2006)*** when everything operated at a stellar crossroads of events. Diving into that subterranean space allowed him to catch raw, autonomous “ideas” like fish, granting him room to explore imagery that feels simultaneously otherworldly and rooted in the primal core of nature. *David* frequently communicates in gnomic pronouncements and rhythmic incantations that carry a mantra-like repetition, stripping complex metaphysical concepts down to stark, elemental truths. This meditative, almost prophetic manner of speaking aided by his regimented TM sessions allowed him to “catch the big fish” in his own wildly striking intuition. How a single verse in the Bible gave him the key to his “most spiritual film” ***Eraserhead (1977)***, bringing everything together as a whole still amazes me about how ideas work.

![Mulholland Drive](../media/David-Lynch-blog\mulholland-drive.jpg"){: width="350px" .align-center}
<p align="center"><sub><em>*Mulholland Drive (2001)*</em></sub></p>

Born in Missoula, Montana, and raised across Idaho and Washington, young *David* often accompanied his father, a research scientist for the U.S. Department of Agriculture, into vast timberlands to study tree diseases and insects. There, he absorbed a distinctly Northwestern sensibility: the coexistence of sunlit, towering pine forests and the rotting underbelly festering just beneath the bark, just like we see in the opening sequence of ***Blue Velvet (1986)***, where idyllic American suburban life full of picket fences with roses is contrasted with a sudden pan to teeming beetles just beneath the green grass. *David* and his brother once saw a naked woman wandering out of their childhood Spokane neighborhood. This terrifying sight, burned into his adult memory, taught him early on the marriage of horror and normalcy informing his hellish tendencies, most famously the visual of a naked and battered *Dorothy Vallens* on a front lawn in ***Blue Velvet (1986)***. Another alter ego of early *Lynch* would be the *Reagan*-era naivety of young adult *Jeffrey Beaumont* on the verge of discovering the timelessly cruel world lurking underneath Americana.

![Blue Velvet](../media/David-Lynch-blog\blue-velvet(4).png"){: width="350px" .align-center}
<p align="center"><sub><em>*Blue Velvet (1986)* opening shot</em></sub></p>

As a young painter studying at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts in fear-soaked, late-1960s Philadelphia, he experienced the epiphany that turned him toward cinema. Staring late one night at a dark painting of garden plants, he suddenly perceived a wind rustling through the canvas and saw the painted foliage begin to stir. “Oh, a moving painting,” he recalled thinking - an impulse that birthed his sculpted screen installation ***Six Men Getting Sick (1967)*** and forever defined his approach to film as living painting and sound. This transitional period, characterized by his early short films, became the primal laboratory for his surrealist sensibilities, which are not so methodically distant from *Buñuel* and *Dalí*’s ***Un Chien Andalou (1929)***. However, *David*’s quintessential dream logic got gradually remote from the effortless satirical surrealism of late-career *Buñuel*. In ***The Alphabet (1968)***, inspired by his niece’s night terrors, *Lynch* fused jagged animation with unsettling live-action to manifest the visceral dread of learning - reducing language and structure to a nightmarish, bodily abstraction. This instinct to physicalize psychological states matured in ***The Grandmother (1970)***, where a neglected boy literally grows a loving matriarch from a seed planted in a bed of soil on a mattress. Arbitrary weirdness could, unironically, be a fraction in the cosmic dread of all proportions.

![RAbbits](../media/Rabbits-wide.jpg"){: width="350px" .align-center}
<p align="center"><sub><em>*Rabbits (2002)*</em></sub></p>

In the history of American experimental cinema, only *Maya Deren* whose silent 1943 trance film ***Meshes of the Afternoon (1943)*** similarly rejected narrative literacy in favor of dream logic, recurring keys, uncanny domestic thresholds, and fractured Los Angeles identities feels like a true spiritual precursor. Once you stop demanding that *Lynch*’s images decode into neat metaphors, his surrealism reveals itself as a pure function of creative joy. Rewatching ***Wild at Heart (1990)***, the random unhinged jolts of shock and grotesquerie are what appeal to me most, not because they conceal a secret motif but because they crackle with the electricity of an unfiltered idea. Beyond meaning and symbolism, the primitive plethora of ideas, however random the execution may seem, suffices (for me) to prove his filmmaking chops that transcend his ethos, as we see clearly in ***The Straight Story (1999)*** (his most experimental one, given it was with Disney). I happily contend that the evil and violence *Lynch* usually portrays are merely projections on his artistic canvas. The surrealist art movement as a whole has become my understanding of heightened creativity; not an arbitrary collage of odd images, but a faculty of instinctive deduction in which strangeness is the by-product of fidelity to an idea. The equation, I suspect, runs both ways. Hovering over it all is *Lynch*’s lifelong touchstone, *Victor Fleming*’s ***The Wizard of Oz (1939)***, a film he once confessed crossed his mind every single day. *Alexandre O. Philippe*’s documentary ***Lynch/Oz (2022)*** asks whether every road in his filmography leads back to the Emerald City: the ruby shoes, the billowing curtains, the singer named *Dorothy* in ***Blue Velvet (1986)***.


The Sound of the Dream
========
For all the critical ink spilled over *Lynch*’s impenetrable narratives, the sonic architecture of his films remains one of his most underrated triumphs. Sound and music in a *Lynch* film do not merely accompany the image; they dictate its atmospheric pressure. One of my favorite cinematic moments of all time is *Ben*’s apartment scene in ***Blue Velvet (1986)***. *Dean Stockwell*, as the rouged and unforgettable *Ben*, mimes *Roy Orbison*’s “In Dreams” into a work light, his face glowing like a carnival ghoul’s, a joker’s mask.

![Blue Velvet](../media/David-Lynch-blog\blue-velvet(2).png"){: width="350px" .align-center}
<p align="center"><sub><em>*Dean Stockwell* in *Blue Velvet (1986)*</em></sub></p>

*“A candy-colored clown they call the sandman*
*Tiptoes to my room every night*
*Just to sprinkle stardust and to whisper*
*"Go to sleep, everything is alright"*
*I close my eyes, then I drift away*
*Into the magic night, I softly say*
*A silent prayer like dreamers do*
*Then I fall asleep to dream my dreams of you*
*In dreams, I walk with you*
*In dreams, I talk to you*
*In dreams, you're mine, all of the time*
*We're together in dreams, in dreams”*

*Frank Booth* (*Dennis Hopper*), the film’s feral id, watches transfixed, dissolves into tears, and then curdles into rage. It is camp, tender, and terrifying all at once: a lullaby sung in the key of menace. The 1963 ballad of a lover who appears only in sleep becomes a dispatch from the film’s own unconscious. *Orbison* was reportedly dismayed by its use, a reminder that *Lynch* loves to turn pop innocence inside out. The choice of song, and of the particular recording, is a detail he has returned to in his books and interviews, and it is never incidental. *Orbison* returns fifteen years later in ***Mulholland Drive (2001)***. At Club Silencio, a magician announces that there is no band, that it is all a tape. Then *Rebekah Del Rio* sings “Llorando,” an a cappella Spanish “Crying,” and collapses while her voice carries on. The trick is exposed, and the tears come anyway: artifice laid bare, feeling undiminished. *Del Rio* reportedly recorded the song in a single take at *Lynch*’s studio, and he wrote her into the film. The same film’s chilling casting scene where a young actress *Camilla Rhodes* forced upon director *Adam Kesher* by menacing studio backers, steps up to a vintage microphone and perfectly lip-syncs to *Linda Scott*’s bouncy 1961 hit “I’ve Told Every Little Star” is another sequence I keep returning to; a bubblegum detour from the imminent sinister corporate mandate: “This is the girl.”

“Cinema is sound and picture both - 50/50 really,” *David Lynch* once said. His sound design (the industrial hum, the low drone, the hush before a cut) carries as much dread as any image. He began as a sound-maker too, co-creating the eerily atmospheric ***Eraserhead (1977)*** soundtrack. *Angelo Badalamenti*, whom *Lynch* first hired to coach *Isabella Rossellini*’s singing on ***Blue Velvet (1986)*** (a rendition so unmistakably doused in the embodiment of *Dorothy Vallens*’ suffering), became his great collaborator, delivering the recognizable, brooding ***Twin Peaks (1990)*** themes. A classic director-composer relationship gave us the tragic melody of “Laura Palmer’s Theme” amidst the dark, wind-swept aesthetics of Pacific Northwest forests. *Badalamenti*’s score acts as the town’s subconscious. *Julee Cruise*’s ethereal vocals, beginning with “Mysteries of Love,” render a landscape of nostalgic warmth and cosmic dread. *Lynch*’s multisensory approach to art has reached popular icons like *Lana Del Rey*, whose “born out of another time” identity has Lynchian elements all over her music, including a 2012 cover of “Blue Velvet” (with an H&M ad video) and ***Twin Peaks: Fire Walk with Me (1992)*** references in the track “Sad Girl” from her album *Ultraviolence*. *Lynch* also acknowledged her song “Video Games” in one of his internet videos. *Chris Isaak*’s “Wicked Game,” which appeared in *Lynch* and *Barry Gifford*’s ***Wild at Heart (1990)***, is perhaps another song that has been popular on TikTok and Reels (*Lana* also performed with *Isaak* recently). Somehow both twangy and gothic, with its deep sense of longing and loss in the film, the song was not only a big hit for *Isaak* but struck the core musical chord for *Nic Cage*’s unhinged charisma. The vulnerability hidden in *Sailor Ripley*’s snakeskin-jacket bravado is anchored by the track’s mixed melody of danger and romance. Without these ever-present soundscapes, *Lynch*’s filmography would have never permeated our subconscious the way it has.

The Architecture of the Unconscious
========
The Freudian current runs strong in *David*’s films, and ***Blue Velvet (1986)*** practically flaunts it. *Jeffrey Beaumont*, a college boy home for the summer, hides in *Dorothy*’s closet and watches *Frank* assault her: a primal scene in the strictest sense, a child witnessing the violence of the parental coupling. *Frank* styles himself “Daddy,” *Dorothy* is at once seductress and wounded mother, and the Oedipal triangle is almost schematic. Critics from *Laura Mulvey* to *Slavoj Žižek* have mined it at length. Even the film’s shape feels analytic: it opens and closes on the same blue curtain, a circularity that recalls *Sigmund Freud*’s notion of an interminable analysis. The later films push further. ***Lost Highway (1997)*** has been mapped onto *Freud*’s structural model, with *Fred*, *Pete*, and the *Mystery Man* read as ego, id, and superego; and ***Mulholland Drive (2001)*** can be read as an extended wish-fulfillment dream, *Diane*’s consoling fantasy precisely the mechanism *Freud* described in *The Interpretation of Dreams*.

![Blue Velvet](../media/David-Lynch-blog\blue-velvet(3).png"){: width="350px" .align-center}
<p align="center"><sub><em>*Isabella Rossellini* in *Blue Velvet (1986)*</em></sub></p>

What distinguishes *Lynch* from the classicists is that he never underlines it. *Alfred Hitchcock* was a deliberate popularizer of *Freud*. ***Spellbound (1945)*** builds its plot around psychoanalysis, complete with a dream sequence designed by *Salvador Dalí*, and ***Psycho (1960)*** ends with a psychiatrist neatly diagnosing *Norman Bates* in a house often read as a map of id, ego, and superego, from fruit cellar to parlor to “mother’s bedroom upstairs.” *Hitchcock* hands the audience the key at the end. *Lynch* pockets it. Of ***Inland Empire (2006)***, he offered only four words: “A woman in trouble.” Perhaps that is the point. *Freud*, for *Lynch*, is not an interpretive device he applies but a climate he breathes: for example, the unconscious as a native language for the burden of alienated identities against the brutal machinery of Hollywood, as we see in his L.A. trilogy. Dreams and the fractured psyche ultimately shatter the veneers of respectability with nightmarish fantasies.

`“\[*Lynch*\] was drawn to mystery because he understood mystery as a conversation - a collision of differences, interpretations, perspectives. Not a message sent down from an all-knowing source.” – *Kyle MacLachlan*, The New York Times`


*Lynch*’s mastery of defamiliarizing nostalgia turns the comforting diner booths and the suburban lawns into a disturbing phantasmagoria which reaches cosmic dimensions in the notorious episode 8 of ***Twin Peaks: The Return (2017)***, where the 1945 Trinity nuclear test visually births absolute evil. How he instilled a space of belonging among us with his most alienating art is very cool, even largely in the utmost nondiegetic fashion. *Lynch*’s depravity is not the gleeful gore of the exploitation shops; it is more intimate and therefore more disturbing, a violation of the sacred space in which we store our nostalgia. Still, the image of the white picket fences with red roses nodding against the cerulean sky of idyllic suburban America is forever burnt into my head, like a stubborn longing for a specific “happiness” (exactly how *David* used to repeat that word). And still I catch myself wanting the roses, wanting the fireman’s wave to mean precisely what it says and let the beetles have their say. *Lynch*, for all his menace and nightmares, never sneers at that wanting. Perhaps that is the quiet genius of the opening. It teaches us that being disenchanted is not the same as being cured of longing.

![eraserhead](../media/David-Lynch-blog\eraserhead.jpg"){: width="350px" .align-center}
<p align="center"><sub><em>A still from *Eraserhead (1977)*</em></sub></p>

There is a classical essence of transgressive art in his mystery, sometimes a liberating form of imagination and always frames of timeless beauty of the uncertain. Our overinvestment in narrative complicates the contemplative consciousness that would confront the personal and collective shadow. The purity of *David Lynch*’s art life extends well beyond the paintings that move and the origin of his ideas as the only sequitur element. His apparent indifference to the movements and theoretical postures perhaps makes his language so universal, especially in examining the subconscious pull between the strange and the familiar; not necessarily a dichotomy of light and dark. *David* knew that these contradictions, so integral to his own art, are at the core of our humanity.
